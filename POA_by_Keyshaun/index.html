Principles of Accounts

Complete Learning Guide — Enhanced with Practical Examples

Master accounting fundamentals, financial statements, partnerships, companies, and internal controls. Build career-ready skills in finance and business.

Foundations of Accounting

Principles of Accounts helps you understand how businesses track, manage, and interpret money. It focuses on how financial information is recorded and used to make sound decisions. The subject builds a strong foundation for careers in business, finance, and accounting.


WHY ACCOUNTING MATTERS
A small bakery uses accounting to see if it is making a profit or loss each month. It supports planning (buying new ovens), control (tracking cash to prevent theft), and overall decision-making while promoting financial responsibility.

Skills you will develop: understanding accounting terminology, applying double-entry recording, analyzing financial data, and making informed judgments. For example, comparing profit across years helps decide whether to expand operations.

Internal control systems protect assets, ensure record accuracy, and reduce fraud. Common controls include separation of duties (cash handler vs. cash recorder) and regular audits.

Examination format: multiple-choice questions, structured problem-solving, and a school-based assessment. Each part tests knowledge, application skills, and analytical reasoning.

Core Topics — Illustrated with Examples
01 Accounting Fundamentals

Assets – resources owned (cash, inventory, equipment). Liabilities – debts owed (bank loans, supplier bills). Capital – owner's investment. Revenue – income from sales/services. Expenses – costs to earn revenue (rent, wages).

Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity (Capital)
PRACTICAL EXAMPLE
A business has $10,000 in total assets and $4,000 in liabilities. The owner's capital equals $6,000. This equation must always balance.
02 Double Entry System

Every transaction affects at least two accounts, ensuring records stay balanced and accurate. Debits must equal credits.

WORKED EXAMPLE
Purchase inventory for $1,000 cash.
• Inventory account increases by $1,000 (debit – asset increase).
• Cash account decreases by $1,000 (credit – asset decrease).
03 Books of Original Entry (Journals)

Transactions are first recorded in specialized journals before posting to ledgers.

  • Sales Journal – only credit sales. E.g., Sold goods on credit to ABC Ltd for $500.
  • Purchases Journal – credit purchases. E.g., Bought inventory from XYZ Co for $300.
  • Cash Book – records cash and bank transactions. E.g., Received $200 cash from customer.
  • General Journal – returns, corrections, adjustments. E.g., Returned faulty goods worth $50 to XYZ Co.
04 Ledger Accounts (T-Accounts)

All transactions are grouped by account. Each T-account shows debits (left) and credits (right).

Cash Account (example)
Debit (Money In)Credit (Money Out)
$1,000 (sales)$300 (rent)
$500 (loan)$200 (supplies)
Balance: $1,000

06 Financial Statements

Income Statement (Profit & Loss) – Revenue minus expenses = Net Profit.
Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet) – Shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific date.

INCOME STATEMENT
Revenue: $20,000
Expenses: $12,000
Net Profit: $8,000
BALANCE SHEET EXTRACT
Assets: $50,000
Liabilities: $20,000
Equity: $30,000 (derived from $50,000 - $20,000)
07 Adjustments (Accruals, Prepayments, Depreciation)

Adjusting entries ensure financial statements reflect the true financial position.

  • Depreciation: Allocating asset cost over useful life. Machine costing $10,000 with 5-year life → $2,000 depreciation per year.
  • Accruals: Expenses incurred but not yet paid. Wages owing at month end: $500.
  • Prepayments: Expenses paid in advance. Paid $1,200 annual insurance; each month $100 is expense, the rest is prepaid.
08 Control Systems & Fraud Prevention

Protect assets and ensure reliability of records. Separation of duties, supervision, and audits reduce fraud risk.

REAL-WORLD CONTROL
A retail store requires two employees to count cash at closing: one records the total, the other deposits it in the safe. This separation reduces theft opportunity.
09 Partnership Accounts

Profit-sharing ratios, capital contributions, drawings, and interest on capital are key features.

PROFIT SHARING EXAMPLE
Ali and Ben share profits 60% and 40%. If net profit is $50,000: Ali receives $30,000, Ben receives $20,000. Partners may also have drawings and interest on capital.
10 Company Accounts (Shares, Dividends)

Corporations raise capital through shares and distribute profits as dividends.

SHARE CAPITAL & DIVIDENDS
A company issues 10,000 shares at $5 each → share capital = $50,000. If profit is $20,000 and the board declares a $5,000 dividend, shareholders receive $0.50 per share.
11 Non-Profit Organizations

Clubs and societies use "excess of income over expenditure" instead of profit. Key items: subscriptions, fundraising, grants.

SPORTS CLUB EXAMPLE
Membership fees: $10,000; Rent: $3,000; Fundraising profit: $2,000. Excess of income over expenditure = $9,000 (not called net profit).
12 Manufacturing Accounts

Calculates cost of production for goods manufactured internally.

PRODUCTION COST
Direct materials: $8,000
Direct labor: $5,000
Factory overhead: $2,000
Total production cost = $15,000
If 1,000 units are made, cost per unit = $15.
13 Payroll Accounting

Recording gross wages, statutory deductions (PAYE, NIS/NHI), and net pay to employees.

PAYROLL CALCULATION
Gross wages: $3,000
Deductions: PAYE tax $400, NIS $150, union fees $50
Net pay to employee: $2,400
Employer records gross wages as expense and deductions as liabilities payable to authorities.
Study Framework & Exam Success
EFFECTIVE PRACTICE
  • Work through past paper questions (CXC structured problems).
  • Understand logic behind double entry instead of memorizing.
  • Focus on neat presentation and clear labeling of accounts.
  • Always verify trial balance before preparing financial statements.
KEY SKILLS ASSESSED
  • Knowledge: definitions and accounting rules.
  • Application: record transactions and prepare financial statements.
  • Analysis: interpret ratios and identify errors.
  • Judgment: recommend decisions based on financial data.

Regular practice develops accuracy and speed. The syllabus progresses from basic principles to complex topics like company and manufacturing accounts. Use internal control knowledge to answer case-study questions about fraud prevention.
